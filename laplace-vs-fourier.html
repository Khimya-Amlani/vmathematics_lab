Anatomy of a
Musical Chord

Every chord you've ever heard is really several pure tones, each ringing and dying away at its own rate, added into one messy waveform. Below, we dissect one — and watch Fourier and Laplace transform each act as a detective, pulling the hidden notes back out.

01 the signal

Build a chord out of three dying notes

A real plucked or struck note is not an eternal, pure sine wave — it is a sine wave wrapped in a decaying envelope, e−αt. Set the pitch, loudness, phase and decay rate of three notes below; they'll sum into one composite wave — your "complex musical wave."

02 · fourier

Fourier transform — the frequency detective who searches

The Fourier transform asks one question, over and over, for every possible frequency f: "how much of a pure tone at exactly this frequency is hiding inside x(t)?" It does this by correlating x(t) against test sinusoids and integrating:

X(jω) = ∫₋∞^∞ x(t) e−jωt dt ,   ω = 2πf

Sweep enough frequencies and the ones actually present in the chord light up as tall peaks in |X(jω)| — that's frequency detection. The angle of X at that peak is the phase, and the sharpness of the peak hints at how long that note lingers.

magnitude spectrum |X(jω)| — detected frequencies
phase spectrum ∠X(jω) — detected phase, faded where signal is weak
Notice: Fourier finds the notes by searching — sweeping a dial across every frequency and watching for a spike, like tuning a radio. It never needs to know in advance what's in the signal.
03 · laplace

Laplace transform — the detective who solves instead of searches

Laplace generalises Fourier by testing against e−st instead of e−jωt, where s = σ + jω is a full complex frequency:

X(s) = ∫₀^∞ x(t) e−st dt

For a decaying note A·e−αtcos(ω₀t+φ), the algebra factors cleanly and X(s) has a pair of poles — points where the denominator hits zero — sitting at exactly s = −α ± jω₀. No sweeping required: factor the denominator, and the pitch (ω₀) and the decay rate (α, the "strength"/persistence of that frequency) fall out directly as coordinates.

s-plane — poles reveal pitch (↑ imaginary axis) and decay strength (← real axis)
Reading the plane: a pole close to the vertical jω-axis rings a long time — a strong, sustained frequency. A pole pushed far to the left dies out almost instantly — a weak, transient one. Drag a note's decay (α) slider up in section 01 and watch its pole walk left, while its Fourier peak in section 02 gets shorter and wider at the same time — two views of one fact.
Why the jω-axis matters: Fourier is simply Laplace evaluated along σ = 0 — the vertical axis of the s-plane. If every pole sits strictly to the left of that axis (every note truly decays, α>0), the Fourier integral converges nicely. A note with α = 0 pushes its pole onto the axis — a tone that never dies, which is exactly why a perfectly pure, endless sine wave gives Fourier textbooks trouble (needs a delta function) while Laplace shrugs and just notes the pole sits at Re(s)=0.

Two transforms, one family

05 so what

Why leave the time domain at all?

In the time domain, x(t) is one tangled squiggle — you can't point to "the 392 Hz part" anywhere on that curve. Every equaliser, tuner, filter, synthesizer and stability check works because someone first asked the same question you just asked with the sliders above: which pure frequencies is this made of, how loud is each one, and how long does each one last? That question only has a clean answer in the frequency domain — which is exactly what Fourier and Laplace hand you.

x(t) → X(jω) → X(s) — same question, two lenses
